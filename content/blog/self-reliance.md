_Or how independence slows you down._

I have been using Linux on and off for at least twelve years. There were times when I tried to go all in, only to come crawling back to a Windows install. By now the pendulum is firmly lodged on the side of Linux; my Windows partition was deleted over two years ago. Since then I was able to learn a lot and help others get started with their journey. With the evolution of the ecosystem this became easier and easier, but the most important change was in my approach. I'll try and explain this shift here.

Naturally, there were multiple reasons for the pendulum swings. For one, video games were always a favorite pastime of mine. So when gaming on Linux was made convenient by Steam, moving away from Windows for good became a no-brainer. But why not earlier? Wine, Proton and Bottles were around for a while and you could get most games to run. Still, I never managed to do so. When I tried and eventually failed, I just went back to Windows. This happened again and again. The problem seemed too intimidating and too large of an effort to overcome. Realistically speaking, I was right about the effort, but not because of the inherent complexity of the task. I was simply making it harder than it needed to be.

I got into Linux by way of Arch, I rolled my own desktop environment and customized everything to hell and back. I ripped out what I didn't think I needed to create the perfect, minimal system. Before you ask, of course I used a tiling window manager. The workflows I set up worked great. I just could not do anything outside them, because then I needed to fiddle with the system. When time came to set up gaming and add more complexity on a brittle base, it fell apart.

Why do it this way then? I got started with Linux during my teenage and early adult years. Back then, I strived for self-reliance above everything. While this might come with the territory of growing up and finding yourself, I believe I practiced this to an unhealthy degree. For example, I never asked anyone for help. Even though the web had communities solely dedicated to answering Linux questions, I never reached out. For me the *right* way, was to do it myself, by myself.  It goes without saying that I am not an isolated case. The pattern of not seeking help and focussing on self-reliance is very common in men. Male socialization often involves stigmas around expressing emotion and showing vulnerability. Being a man is equated with strength and rationality, while emotions tend to get associated with the "weaker sex". To show vulnerability is to be weak, is to be unmanly. In reality however, all people feel emotions and require help from time to time. When men do, they are told to suck it up. Experiencing these rejections further reinforces the stigma and men retreat into a shell. It seems the only person one can rely on, is oneself. The formation of these societal values intersect, besides gender, with political views, economics and technology. The adverse effect on mental health is well-studied. I would argue there is also a negative effect on  learning.

To do everything yourself quickly leads to getting sidetracked. If you want to solve a problem in its entirety, there are always more factors to consider than the initial analysis yields. Complex tasks tend to generate more and more related ones. Being in the mindset of solving all aspects of the problem, you probably are tempted to tackle them in one go. The resulting mammoth task is highly complex and requires context switches for its various aspects. This can be avoided by breaking tasks down, of course. However, it goes almost without saying that refusing help means one has to spend more time on a given task, assuming coordination effort is low. Dealing with this higher workload takes more time in comparison, which is what this all boils down too. You move slower, can experiment less and are more easily frustated. All of those outcomes limit learning.

Back to my case. It turns out replacing systems like a desktop environment that entire teams had poured inordinate amounts of time into is not that easy. It takes a very long time to implement and the resulting feature set is minimal. Doing it the *right* way meant reinventing a fair amount of wheels. I was also not aware of other ways of doing things, I only knew my own. This led to a brittle base, that I could not easily build upon. When I tried, I was met with the prospect of putting disparagingly large efforts into getting basic desktop functionality to work. One could argue that I did learn much about Linux this way. While this is true, I can't help but wonder how much faster I would have arrived at the point where I am today if I had done things differently.

I used to think convenience and deep learning were on opposite ends of a spectrum. By now I would argue that this is a false dichotomy. Convenience enables learning.  Purposefully underutilizing ressources such as other's help and ready-made solutions slows you down. In my future I see only jolly cooperation.
